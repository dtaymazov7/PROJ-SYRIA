## Problem Statement

Workshops and small factories in Aleppo's in-city industrial zones (al-Kalasa, al-Arqoub) get about 12 hours of grid power a day and are believed to fill the rest with diesel at roughly $0.40-0.47/kWh. Rooftop solar would cut that cost, but a factory-sized system costs $50k-100k upfront, no Syrian bank lends for it, and owners don't trust the systems will perform. The question for this design: what is the most realistic, NPV-positive business that closes that gap?

The earlier plan (Microgrid YC Validation Pack, Oct 2026) was 15-year power-as-a-service (PaaS): the company owns a $272k site and sells kWh at $0.26. This session replaces it with 36-month lease-to-own, because the PaaS model returns 16% unlevered against a ~25% Syria hurdle and never pays back in its own bad case.

## Demand Evidence

None yet. No factory owner has been interviewed, no LOI exists and no diesel invoice has been seen. Every demand number below is desk research.

Indirect evidence from the Aside landscape search (Oct 2026):
- Syrian rooftop solar adoption is led by factories, malls and hotels; off-grid capacity rose from 931 MW (2023) to 2,060 MW (2025) ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/energy/2026/07/06/syrias-solar-boom-is-redefining-middle-easts-energy-model/)).
- A 2026 field study found factories in Aleppo, Idlib and Raqqa buying panels to run through outages, with industrial systems up to $50k; cost and trust limit uptake ([People in Need](https://www.peopleinneed.net/the-growing-business-of-solar-power-in-northern-syria-and-what-it-needs-to-function-better-13740gp)).
- Most Syrian solar is self-financed; buyers with dollar income went solar, others stayed on generators ([Green Central Banking](https://greencentralbanking.com/2026/09/14/inside-syrias-decentralised-solar-boom)).

This shows the want exists. It does not show that unfunded factories will sign a lease at the modeled price.

## Status Quo

Unverified. The assumed workaround is a factory-owned diesel generator for the off-grid hours, at $1.11-1.30/litre (Enab Baladi, Sept 2026). Some target factories may already have cash-bought panels, and some may buy from ampere operators. The first interviews must establish which.

## Target User & Narrowest Wedge

Target user: the owner of a daytime-shift workshop or small factory in al-Kalasa or al-Arqoub who wants solar, has the roof, but cannot pay $50k-100k upfront. No individual is named yet.

Narrowest wedge: one 100 kWp + 100 kWh system, sold at landed cost plus 8%, with 25% down and 36 monthly USD-indexed payments, remote lockout, and ownership passing to the factory at month 36. The markup is 8%, below an installer's 15-20% cash margin, because the return comes from the lease rate; a higher markup would make leasing dearer than buying for cash. Before any financing, the wedge can start as a plain cash or deposit-backed sale through a local installer, which earns margin and meets customers with no credit risk.

## Constraints

- Founders are Stanford students; no founder is in Syria yet and no local partner is named.
- Sanctions: comprehensive US sanctions are gone (EO 14312, Caesar Act repealed, SST removed Aug 2026), but each bill-of-materials item needs an export-control classification; inverters and modems with encryption are the risk.
- Currency: Syrian pound revenue vs. USD equipment. Leases are USD-indexed, which shifts devaluation pain to the factory (see Zimbabwe PPA case, [Africa Energy Portal](https://africa-energy-portal.org/news/zimbabwe-currency-chaos-chokes-zimbabwes-power-producers)).
- Capital: a loss-tolerant pilot pool of at most $500k.

## Premises

Presented to the founder for confirmation; no disagreement was recorded.

1. The factory's saving is a hypothesis until it shows diesel invoices. The model puts it at 42% of diesel spend during the lease in the base case ($24.6k of $58.1k), negative in the bad case and 62% in the good case.
2. The business is financing plus collections; hardware is a commodity. ecoligo (missed investor payments in 2026 after customer arrears) and Africa GreenTec (preliminary insolvency, June 2026) show collections and funding timing kill PaaS firms.
3. Cash-rich factories already bought panels; the customer is the factory that wants solar but cannot fund it.
4. No capex before a local Aleppo connector, 20 interviews and one signed LOI.
5. A founder must be in Syria for the first pilot.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: 15-year power-as-a-service (prior plan)
Own a $272k, 250 kWp + 500 kWh site and sell kWh at $0.26 for 10-15 years. 16.4% unlevered IRR, below the 25% hurdle, and no payback in the doc's bad case. Rejected: 15 years of exposure to collections, devaluation and grid arrival (Saudi 760 MW solar at ~3c/kWh), and the closest comparables failed in 2026.

### Approach B: 36-month lease-to-own (recommended)
Sell a 100 kWp + 100 kWh system at landed cost plus 8%, 25% deposit, 28% annual USD-indexed lease rate, ownership transfers at month 36. Modeled in `models/lease-to-own-unit-economics.xlsx`.

| Scenario | Capital out per deal | Factory net saving / yr | Financier IRR | NPV @ 25% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | $60.9k | +$24.6k | 28.1% | +$2.1k |
| Bad (capex +20%, 65% self-use, $1.11 diesel, 85% collection) | $75.2k | -$4.4k | 13.0% | -$10.0k |
| Good | $46.7k | +$44.6k | 37.0% | +$6.1k |

Base-case cumulative cash turns positive in month 26. A $500k pool relending collections signs about 33 deals in 5 years (model tab Pilot pool: deals signed at the start of each year with the cash on hand, each deal's 36 months of net collections spread evenly over 3 years, no costs beyond the per-deal lines, losses only through the collection rate). Effort M, risk Med.

How the base case gets from the lease terms to 28.1% (all lines are in the model's Assumptions and Cashflows tabs):

| Line | Base value | Effect per month |
|---|---|---|
| Landed cost | $83.4k (PV $0.34/W, install $0.05/W, battery $180/kWh, controls $6k, freight 10%, security and insurance $4k, engineering $3k, contingency 10%) | paid at month 0 |
| Price to factory | cost + 8% = $90.1k | |
| Deposit | 25% of price = $22.5k, received at month 0 | net cash out $60.9k |
| Scheduled payment | $2,794 (PMT of $67.5k at 28%/yr over 36 months) | +$2,794 |
| Collection rate | 95% | -$140 |
| Recovery on uncollected | 30% of the shortfall | +$42 |
| Servicing and monitoring | $150/month | -$150 |
| O&M visits | $1,500/year | -$125 |
| Net monthly cash | | +$2,421 |

36 x $2,421 = $87.2k back on $60.9k out. That gives 28.1% annual IRR and +$2.1k NPV at 25%. The reviewer's +$11-12k figure assumed 100% collection and no servicing or O&M.

Bad and good cost and lease inputs: PV $0.408 / $0.30 per W, install $0.06 / $0.04 per W, battery $216 / $160 per kWh, markup 5% / 10%, deposit 25% / 30%, lease rate 28% / 30%, collection 85% / 98%, recovery 10% / 40%, servicing $200 / $120 a month, O&M $2,000 / $1,200 a year.

Known gap: the $4k security and insurance line covers fencing and the first year's premium only. Years 2-3 of insurance (about $1k a year, estimated) are not in the model; adding them cuts the base NPV from +$2.1k to about +$0.9k.

Energy assumptions per scenario: yield 1,650 / 1,600 / 1,700 kWh per kWp per year (base / bad / good); share of output that displaces diesel 80% / 65% / 85%; diesel $1.30 / $1.11 / $1.40 per litre at 0.30 / 0.28 / 0.32 litres per kWh, plus $0.05 / $0.03 / $0.05 per kWh of avoided generator O&M.

The 80% base share is conditional. It assumes the factory's grid-off hours fall mostly in daylight, so solar replaces diesel rather than grid power. If the 12 grid hours land during the working day, solar mostly offsets the grid tariff, which is unknown here and probably cheaper than diesel. Then the saving collapses toward the bad case. This is the first thing each interview must establish (see the Phase 2 entry gate).

The "factory net saving" column is the saving during the lease, net of lease payments. After month 36 the factory keeps the full avoided diesel cost (about $58k a year in the base case), less its own maintenance.

### Approach C: Cash installer / distributor
Sell and install for cash with a deposit. 15-20% gross margin, positive from the first job, no credit risk, but crowded and with no moat. Used as phase 1 of B rather than as the business.

## Recommended Approach

Approach B, entered through C.

0. Gate 0 (before anything is sourced or sold): a named Aleppo installer partner is signed up; 20 interviews and one LOI are done (Premise 4); and a sanctions lawyer has classified every bill-of-materials item, including the inverter, battery management system and lockout modem, as exportable to Syria (EAR99 or covered by License Exception SPP). Until all three are met, no inventory or tools are bought, no capex is spent, and the founders place no purchase orders, payments or shipments. Choosing candidate models and requesting quotes is allowed before Gate 0, since the lawyer needs a concrete bill of materials. Target: Gate 0 passed by 2027-01-31. All later months count from the day Gate 0 is passed.
1. Phase 1, after Gate 0, about 3 months: partner-only cash sales. The named installer partner runs sales and installs, and buys equipment only against customer deposits, so the company holds no inventory and spends no capex. The founders handle sourcing, quotes and interviews remotely. Every partner purchase must be on the Gate 0 classification list; an item found not exportable is replaced by a classified alternative, never shipped. At every factory visit, including factories that do not buy, the partner logs the factory's grid-on and grid-off hours by time of day, generator run-hours and diesel litres from invoices. Shortlisted factories also get a one-week clamp-meter load logger (about $200). The displacement share is derived by matching the factory's hourly load (average generator load = monthly litres / 0.30 litres per kWh / run-hours, checked against the logger) against the hourly output of 100 kWp of solar and the 100 kWh battery during grid-off hours. Goal: real diesel data, delivered quotes, and a list of owners who said no only because of cash.
2. Phase 2, months 3-12: offer the 36-month lease to factories that pass the Phase 2 entry gate below. Funded by a pilot pool under $500k or a donor or DFI facility (the Lebanon SoLR& model, [Arab Reform](https://www.arab-reform.net/publication/unlocking-solar-energy-access-in-lebanon-through-innovative-finance-and-blended-models/)). Premise 5 applies here: a founder is in Aleppo before the first lease is signed. Phase 1 can run without a founder on the ground because the partner carries no financing risk for the company.
3. After 12 months of repayment data: raise DFI debt against the book and scale.

Phase 2 entry gate, for each candidate factory:
- Diesel price and litres per month taken from invoices, not estimates.
- Measured share of solar output that would displace diesel, from the Phase 1 hour logs, of at least 60%. That is the break-even at $1.11 diesel plus a margin at $1.30.
- A modeled net saving during the lease of at least 20% of current diesel spend, using that factory's own numbers in the model.
- A written legal opinion that the lease and repossession are enforceable in Aleppo.
- The equipment for this factory is on the Gate 0 export-control classification list; any new item gets classified before it ships.

## Lease Operations

Remote lockout policy:
- Trigger: a payment more than 15 days late.
- Notice: written notice on days 1, 7 and 14 by WhatsApp and paper, through the local agent.
- Scope: the inverter stops exporting solar and battery power at the end of the factory's shift. It never cuts a running load mid-shift, and the factory falls back to its own generator, which stays installed.
- Restore: within 24 hours of payment.
- Transfer: at the final payment (month 36, or later if restructured), the lockout is permanently disabled and the factory receives the inverter admin credentials.
- Dependency: the lockout depends on a networked modem and inverter firmware, which are classified at Gate 0 before any sourcing, and a legal opinion that disconnection is lawful under the lease (Phase 2 gate).

Default and recovery:
1. Days 1-30: notices and lockout as above, then an offer to restructure (extend the term by up to 6 months).
2. Days 30-60, if restructuring is declined: the lockout stays on, the restructure offer stays open, and a final written notice goes out on day 45.
3. Day 60 with no payment: terminate the lease, remove panels, battery and inverter, and redeploy or resell them. The financier pays removal and transport, budgeted at about $3k a deal.
4. The model's 30% base recovery (10% bad, 40% good) is resale value recovered on the uncollected balance, net of the $3k removal cost. The 85% bad-case collection rate is before that recovery. Payment reductions under the performance commitment are not modeled separately; they sit inside the collection rate.
5. Repossession feasibility is a Phase 2 gate. If the legal opinion says equipment cannot be removed, the recovery assumption goes to zero and the lease rate or deposit must rise.

O&M, warranty and insurance:
- During the lease, the financier owns the system, services it (the $1,500/yr O&M line) and insures it against theft and damage (the $4k security and insurance line covers year 1; years 2-3 are the unmodeled gap noted above). Conflict damage is excluded unless political-risk cover is found.
- Performance commitment: if monthly output falls more than 20% below the modeled yield because of equipment failure, that month's payment is reduced in proportion until the fault is repaired. Manufacturer warranties (typically 10-12 years on panels, 5-10 on LFP batteries) are assigned to the factory at transfer.
- After the final payment: the factory owns the system, carries maintenance and insurance, and may buy an optional paid service plan.

Why B: capital is exposed for 3 years, not 15; the deposit screens buyers; remote lockout is real leverage over a short lease; and grid arrival after transfer is the factory's problem. Grid hours rising during the lease are a default-risk driver: every 10 points of self-use lost cuts the factory's saving by about $7.3k a year, and the bad case (65% self-use) already makes the factory lose money. The per-deal NPV is thin (+$2.1k base), so the business works only on volume, fast capital recycling and cheap debt, not on any single deal.

The model's bad case is negative for both sides: the factory loses money, so it will stop paying. The live go/no-go numbers are therefore the share of output that displaces diesel (load profile) and the diesel price, not the capex.

## Open Questions

- Do al-Kalasa factories run their own generators, buy from ampere operators, or already have panels?
- What share of daytime load would solar actually displace? At the base lease payment ($2,794/month), the factory's saving disappears below about 46% self-use at $1.30 diesel, or about 60% at $1.11 diesel.
- Will owners accept a USD-indexed payment, and how do they pay for diesel today (cash, USD, credit)?
- Who is the Aleppo partner, and which founder goes to Syria, when?
- Can a lease be enforced, and can equipment be repossessed, under current Syrian law and practice?
- Which DFI or donor facility (SoLR&-style) would back a Syria pilot pool?

## Success Criteria

- 20 factory interviews with diesel litres, price and generator size logged.
- At least 5 of 20 say yes to a written offer; at least 1 signed LOI at a stated price and system size.
- One delivered-to-Aleppo quote within 10% of the model's $83k landed cost.
- Phase 1: hour logs (grid on/off by time of day, generator hours, diesel litres) for every factory visited, and at least 10 factories passing the Phase 2 entry gate.
- After 12 months of leases: collection rate of at least 95%, and the base case holding (factory net saving above zero).

## Dependencies

- A named Aleppo connector (engineer, installer or industrial-committee contact).
- Export-control classification of the bill of materials from a sanctions lawyer.
- A supplier quote delivered to Aleppo (Chinese via Mersin or Beirut, plus one Turkish integrator).
